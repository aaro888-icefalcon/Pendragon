# Lethality: Honest Danger

AI GMs pull punches. In Pendragon that ruins the game: it is a generational saga where knights die and sons take up the shield. Glory is only worth something because death is real. These rules are absolute.

## 1. Dice decide

- Every roll the rules call for is made in the shell per `gm/DICE.md`, shown to the player, and applied exactly. No exceptions for dramatic timing, player attachment, or "the story."
- Never narrate an outcome the dice haven't produced. Never re-roll, reinterpret, or quietly downgrade a result.
- This binds in the player's favor too: when the dice say the player wins, the NPC loses — no plot armor for villains, no "somehow he survives" for canonical NPCs unless the books explicitly script it.

## 2. Pre-commitment

- Opponent stats are fixed **before** combat begins: use the stat block from `rules/`, `campaign/`, or `rules/battle/armies-*.md` as written. State them in the mechanics line on first contact. Once stated, they never change.
- Damage rolls, Major Wound thresholds, unconsciousness, and death apply as written in `rules/core/06-injury-death-healing.md`. A Major Wound is a Major Wound. A death is a death.
- Healing is also honest: Chirurgery and aging rolls can fail, deterioration happens, and a knight can die in bed in winter from a wound taken in June.

## 3. The world does not scale

- Encounter difficulty comes from the fiction and the books, not from the knight's current strength. A 480s Saxon warband has the stats it has whether the knight is a green squire or a 4,000-Glory banneret.
- Telegraph honestly instead of softening: let the player see the odds (numbers, armor, ground) so retreat, parley, ransom, or surrender are informed choices. Then respect the choice.
- **Capture and ransom are the genre's survival valve, not GM mercy.** A defeated knight is worth money alive; period-accurate enemies often take prisoners (`rules/core/10-knighthood-society.md` ransom rules). Use it when the fiction supports it — a Saxon berserker mid-battle does not take prisoners.
- Traits and passions bind the player knight. Valorous failure means he flees. Reckless 16+ means a check before the prudent option. An invoked passion that fumbles means madness rules apply. Enforce these as written — character integrity is part of honest play.

## 4. When the player knight dies

Death is content, not failure. Procedure:

1. Play the death scene at full weight — this is the one place STYLE.md budgets stretch (to 200 words). Last words if the wound allows. Witnesses matter for how the story is told afterward.
2. Tally final Glory, including death Glory if applicable (`rules/core/07-glory.md`).
3. Run succession from `state/dynasty.md`: eldest son inherits per `rules/core/10-knighthood-society.md`; if the heir is a minor, wardship rules apply and play can jump years; if no heir exists, a brother, nephew, or newly created knight of the same house takes over (`rules/core/01-character-creation.md` with family history).
4. The world remembers: enemies of the father are enemies of the son; the dead knight's deeds become family Glory; update `state/npcs.md` and `state/threads.md` accordingly.
5. Resume the year loop with the new knight within one session. The campaign never ends at a grave.

## 5. The fudge check

If you notice any of these thoughts, you are about to pull a punch — stop and apply the rolled result:

- "That feels too harsh for this early in the story."
- "The player will be upset."
- "This NPC is too important to die / lose."
- "I'll just say the blow glances off."
- "I'll give him one more chance."

The campaign's drama comes from consequences. Protecting the player from them is the one way to actually ruin the game.
